How the math works

Removing the vig. A book's listed price implies a probability higher than the real one — that gap is their margin. If you enter the opposing side's price too, the app splits the two implied probabilities so they sum to 100%, which strips out the vig and reveals the book's genuine estimate. Without the other side, it assumes a typical 4.5% two-way margin, which is a rough guess.

Edge. Your estimate minus the no-vig probability. Positive means you think the book is wrong in your favour. This number is only as good as your estimate — that's the hard part, and no app can do it for you.

Expected value. Your combined probability multiplied by the payout, minus the chance of losing your stake. Negative EV means that over many repetitions of this exact bet you lose money, however good it feels.

Stake sizing. Quarter-Kelly: a quarter of the mathematically optimal fraction. Full Kelly is correct only if your probability estimates are perfect, which they never are, so fractional Kelly protects you from your own overconfidence.

Correlation. Multiplying probabilities assumes legs are independent. Two legs from the same game rarely are — "over 2.5 goals" and "both teams to score" move together. Books know this and price same-game parlays with extra margin, so the app flags them rather than pretending the math is clean.

A calculator, not a tipster. It has no odds feed and no opinion about who wins — it only checks your own numbers for internal consistency. Sports betting carries real financial risk, and parlays carry more of it than single bets.
